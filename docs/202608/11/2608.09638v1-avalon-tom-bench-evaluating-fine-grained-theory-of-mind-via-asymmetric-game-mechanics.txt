Abstract 

Theory of Mind (ToM) is essential for agent interactions, yet existing evaluations either rely on static scenarios that oversimplify mental-state reasoning or interactive settings that pro-vide limited diagnostic insight. We present 

Avalon-ToM-Bench , a fine-grained bench-mark that operationalizes ToM through the asymmetric-information mechanics of The Re-sistance: Avalon . Rather than evaluating end-to-end gameplay, Avalon-ToM-Bench decom-poses ToM into a 2 × 2 taxonomy spanning 

epistemic versus motivational reasoning and 

inference versus action , using human-crafted, perspective-constrained queries. Benchmark-ing 28 LLMs reveals three insights: First , mod-els show strong game-rule comprehension but weaker ToM abilities. Second , mechanistic analyses via linear probing and activation steer-ing demonstrate that models frequently rep-resent correct mental-state inferences in their hidden states but fail to express them during generation (linear probes recover 77–82% ac-curacy versus 62–70% from the models’ own chain-of-thought), indicating that ToM limita-tions are primarily a problem of expression rather than representation. Third , dedicated reasoning training yields substantial improve-ments whereas test-time chain-of-thought pro-vides only marginal gains ( +11 .0 versus +1 .1

points on average), suggesting that robust ToM depends on a learned reasoning policy rather than increased inference-time deliberation. 1

1 Introduction

However, prevailing methodologies often miss this nuance.

Traditional benchmarks (Wu et al., 2023; Shinoda et al., 2025; Gandhi et al., 2023) frequently probe superficial patterns through static, overly simplistic scenarios—such as explicitly querying a character’s emotional state or visual perspective— stripping away the asymmetric information, deception, and multi-party dynamics inherent to real-world social reasoning. On the other hand, while hidden-role games offer rich, interactive en-vironments (Serrino et al., 2019; Light et al., 2023, 2025; Rahimirad et al., 2026), evaluating models strictly on end-to-end performance (e.g., win rates or action prediction) sacrifices interpretability. Such outcome-driven metrics blur the lines between genuine mind-reading and orthogonal skills like strategic planning, persuasion, or logical memory, making it impossible to isolate true ToM deficits from broader statistical biases. 1arXiv:2608.09638v1 [cs.AI] 10 Aug 2026 To address these limitations, we introduce 

Avalon-ToM-Bench , utilizing the mechanics of 

The Resistance: Avalon as a controlled diagnostic environment rather than a gameplay arena. We map ToM across two fundamental axes: the nature of the targeted mental state ( Epistemic knowledge versus Motivational intent) and its cognitive ex-ecution (passive Inference versus active Action ). This yields four distinct quadrants of asymmetric-information reasoning. To guarantee objective as-sessment, Avalon-ToM-Bench queries models us-ing highly constrained, human-verified proposi-tions that restrict available evidence exclusively to the designated player’s point of view and the public interaction history, penalizing models that rely on omniscient context. We further verify that these axes are empirically dissociable: under permuta-tion testing, item difficulty loads on the epistemic dimension whereas the benefit of chain-of-thought loads on the orthogonal inference–action dimen-sion, confirming that the taxonomy carves ToM at meaningful joints rather than imposing an arbitrary partition. By cleanly decoupling ToM from general game-playing heuristics, Avalon-ToM-Bench is, to the best of our knowledge, the first framework to operationalize mental-state reasoning as a multi-dimensional, testable skill. We benchmark 28 distinct LLMs—encompassing various sizes, rea-soning paradigms, and open/closed weights— uncovering three primary insights: • Rule Mastery Does Not Equal Social Intel-ligence: Models struggle with perspective-constrained ToM (performance 50 − 60% )despite recalling Avalon’s mechanics near-perfectly (average rule comprehension accu-racy ≈85%). • The Latent Competence Gap: Linear prob-ing and activation steering reveal that models internally compute the correct deduction but fail to surface it—probes recover 77–82% ac-curacy versus 62–70% from the model’s own chain-of-thought, suggesting that ToM failure may be one of expression, not representation. • Robust ToM Requires Learned Policies, Not Just Deliberation Test-time chain-of-thought yields volatile, marginal gains ( +1 .1

pts), whereas reasoning-trained models im-prove consistently ( +11 .0 pts)—robust ToM depends on a learned reasoning policy, not more inference-time tokens. 

2 Related Work 

Theory of Mind and its Evaluation in LLMs. 

Early LLM studies treated ToM (Premack and Woodruff, 1978) as a monolithic capability probed by short, bias-controlled question-answering (Le et al., 2019). Later benchmarks broadened coverage—conversational asymmetry (Kim et al., 2023), long-form narratives (Xu et al., 2024a), fine-grained behaviors (Chen et al., 2024), and higher-order reasoning (Wu et al., 2023)—but still frame ToM as passive reading and entangle epistemic knowledge with motivational intent. Adversarial stress tests expose its brittleness (Shapira et al., 2024; Ullman, 2023), yet why models fail, and how to mitigate it, remains open. 

Social Deduction Games as Reasoning Envi-ronments. Hidden-role games are long-standing testbeds for reasoning under information asymme-try, from pre-LLM game-theoretic agents (Serrino et al., 2019; FAIR et al., 2022) to a fast-growing line of LLM players (Light et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Light et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2024b; Rahimi-rad et al., 2026). These validate belief inference only through end-to-end game success, conflating it with planning, persuasion, and memory. Game-based evaluations instead target strategic compe-tence (Duan et al., 2024; Lu, 2025) or score behav-ioral alignment to human play (Song et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025); either way their gold labels are behavioral rather than the objective correctness of a mental-state deduction. 

Probing and Steering Internal Representations. 

A complementary interpretability line reads and writes model computation directly: linear probes recover latent task information (Alain and Bengio, 2017; Belinkov, 2022), models encode knowledge their generations omit (Burns et al., 2023; Azaria and Mitchell, 2023), and activation steering shows such directions are causal (Turner et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2023). We bring these tools to ToM to show that mental-state inferences are internally repre-sented yet under-expressed—a mechanistic axis unseen in prior ToM benchmarks. In Appendix C we discuss related work in detail.

3.1 A 2 × 2 Taxonomy of Theory of Mind 

We organize ToM along two axes. The first is the 

nature of the mental state being reasoned about: 

Epistemic —what a seat (a player’s role-defined vantage point) can perceive or know (beliefs, in-formation access)—versus Motivational —what a seat wants or intends (goals, motives). The second is how that mental state is engaged : Inference ,where the target mental state exists from the role and board structure and must be read off with no communicative act involved, versus Action , where a strategic act—a public claim, a sabotage choice— is itself the object of reasoning and must be pro-duced or interpreted as a deliberate move. Cross-ing the two axes yields four dissociable abilities, each of which is naturally instantiated by a distinct 

Avalon scenario (Figure 3). 

Perspective-Taking (Epistemic × Inference). 

The Merlin–Percival–Morgana structure makes ad-versarial roles deliberately confusable (Figure 3); the task is to read off what a given seat can legit-imately know under these constraints—a purely epistemic inference with no strategic act. 

Strategic Signaling (Epistemic × Action). The 

Lady of the Lake holder privately learns a player’s faction and then makes a public, unverifiable claim; the evidence is epistemic but the object of reason-ing is a communicative act —revealing, concealing, faking, or decoding that claim. 

Intention Attribution (Motivational × Infer-ence). Suspicion redirection scenarios reveal no identity directly; the model must abduce a hidden 

motive from an observed action. 

Tacit Coordination (Motivational × Action). 

The EVIL sabotage protocol (priority Assassin 

> Morgana > Mordred; Appendix D.4) requires EVIL teammates to converge, without communica-tion , on who plays the FAIL card—a coordinated 

act that must mesh with teammates’ shared intent. 3.2 Perspective-Constrained Task Formulation 

Let R denote the Avalon ruleset, G the complete game state, H the public interaction history, and 

s a candidate inference. Most items designate a player i, either through an explicit perspective in-struction Pi or directly in the target query. The role-visibility rules induce an information projec-tion Ii = Π i(G) containing only what player i can legitimately know, and the model must evaluate s

using evidence restricted to this projection: 

ˆy = M (R, P i, G, H, s ), ˆy ∈ { TRUE , FALSE }.

Although the model is shown the complete state 

G, valid evidence is confined to Ii and H; it must reconstruct the player’s epistemic state rather than treat every stated identity as accessible knowledge. A role-conditioned statement is labeled T RUE when it is valid under R, Ii, H, and any explicit sce-nario assumptions, and F ALSE if it violates the rules, relies on inaccessible information, or makes an unsupported belief or strategic inference. The evidence and judgment criteria specific to each di-mension are detailed in Appendix E, and one fully worked item per dimension is given in Appendix J. Statements from the same scenario are evaluated 

independently rather than as competing multiple-choice options. A small number of EVIL-Protocol items are observer-level : they test whether a proto-col consequence follows from the complete state without assigning a viewpoint, and are assessed directly against R, G, and H.

Evaluation. Each instance presents the game setup, relevant history, and one target statement; the gold label and reference rationale are with-held from the model, which outputs a single TRUE /F ALSE judgment. We report accuracy and, because some diagnostic subsets are class-skewed, macro-averaged F1, which exposes any residual TRUE /F ALSE prior. All items are run zero-shot with a fixed system/user prompt template (Ap-pendix H); decoding regimes and the full model roster are given in Appendix I.

4 Experiments 

We evaluate 28 models spanning proprietary and open-weight families, reasoning-trained and stan-dard instruction-tuned variants, and sizes from 0.6B up. Each is prompted zero-shot with a fixed template—game setup, public history, and one perspective-constrained statement—and returns a TRUE /F ALSE judgment (§3.2). We report accu-racy and macro-F1 per ability on the full balanced set (408 items, 50% T RUE per ability), so a resid-ual T RUE /F ALSE prior surfaces as an Acc– F1 gap. A companion rules exam tests recall of Avalon’s mechanics, separating game knowledge from rea-soning about minds.

4.2 The Taxonomy Carves ToM at Dissociable Joints 

The per-ability ordering could reflect an arbitrary difficulty ranking. To test whether the two axes of our taxonomy (§3.1) truly capture distinct struc-ture, we decompose two per-cell quantities over the 2 × 2 grid—item difficulty (mean accuracy) and the benefit of chain-of-thought (§5.2)—into main effects for the Epistemic/Motivational axis and the Inference/Action axis, and assess each effect with a permutation test that shuffles cell labels. The two quantities load on orthogonal axes (Table 2). Difficulty is driven by the Epis-temic/Motivational axis ( −8.5 pp, p < 0.001 ): epistemic abilities (Perspective-Taking, Strategic Signaling), which demand reconstructing what an-other seat can perceive, are reliably harder, while the Inference/Action axis has no significant effect on difficulty ( −3.2 pp, p = 0 .14 ). The benefit of chain-of-thought shows the mirror-image pattern: it is governed by the Inference/Action axis ( −7.9

pp, p = 0 .008 )—deliberation helps the Action abil-ities, where a strategic move must be produced or decoded, more than the Inference abilities—while the Epistemic axis has no significant effect ( −1.2

pp, p = 0 .71 ). This double dissociation confirms that the taxonomy partitions ToM along meaning-ful, empirically separable dimensions rather than imposing an arbitrary grouping: what makes an item hard and what deliberation can fix are con-trolled by different axes of the design. A significant difficulty interaction ( −9.3 pp, p = 0 .027 ) further indicates that Perspective-Taking—epistemic and 

inference—is harder than the sum of the two main effects would predict, consistent with it being the single hardest cell. 

5 Ablation Studies 

Beyond ranking models, Avalon-ToM-Bench is de-signed to localize where and why Theory of Mind breaks. We report two analyses, conducted on the open-weight models whose internal states we can access. The first (§5.1) shows that the correct an-swer is present in a model’s internal representation yet under-expressed at its output: ToM failure is largely a problem of expression, not representation .The second (§5.2) shows that test-time chain-of-thought does little to close this gap, whereas ded-icated reasoning training does—implicating the learned reasoning policy rather than the amount of inference-time deliberation. 

5.1 Internal Representations as Stronger Predictors of ToM 

If low accuracy reflected an inability to represent 

the answer, the answer should be as absent from a model’s internal states as from its output. We find the opposite through three distinct perspectives: 

A linear probe beats the model’s own rea-soning. We train logistic probes on the last-token hidden states of the Qwen3 models (every layer, five-fold cross-validation) to predict the cor-rect T RUE /F ALSE label, and compare each probe against the same model’s chain-of-thought output on the identical items. Interestingly, the probe is substantially more accurate ( ≈77–82%) than the model’s own output ( ≈62–70%), and the an-swer becomes linearly decodable already in the 

middle layers (Figure 4). The model computes the ToM answer internally but does not surface it, and the probe–output gap is largest for Perspective-Taking—the hardest ability.

Test-time deliberation barely moves ToM; train-ing moves it a lot. Toggling chain-of-thought on the same Qwen3 weights changes overall accuracy by only +1 .1 pp on average, and the sign is mixed across the size sweep—negative for the 0.6B and 4B models (Table 4a). In contrast, comparing the base Qwen3-4B to its reasoning-trained variant (Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507) yields a much larger and consistent +11 .0 pp overall, up to +20 .6 on Perspective-Taking (Table 4b). Since simply let-ting the base model think longer does not reach this level, the improvement is attributable to the trained reasoning policy rather than to inference-time de-liberation. 

Chain-of-thought is highly unpredictable. The small overall improvement hides a lot of instability: using CoT flips the model’s outcome on ∼27% of the questions (fixing roughly 16% but breaking 12%). Therefore, the modest net gain simply means the fixes and breaks almost cancel each other out, rather than showing a reliable boost (Appendix L, Figure 6). An example of a model talking itself out of the correct answer is provided in Appendix K. 

Longer reasoning does not help either. Nor does the sheer amount of deliberation. Bin-ning each model’s thinking-on responses by length quartile, accuracy does not rise from the shortest to the longest quartile—it declines (pooled corr( length , correct ) = −0.08 ; Ap-pendix M,Figure 7). This is partly confounded, since harder items elicit longer responses, so we claim only that reasoning length is not a lever on ToM performance, not that length causes errors. Test-time chain-of-thought is therefore a weak and unreliable route to ToM: it neither closes the ex-pression gap of §5.1 nor improves systematically with model size or reasoning length. The gains that do materialize come from a better reasoning policy ,learned in training rather than spent at inference. 

6 Conclusions 

We introduced Avalon-ToM-Bench, a benchmark that treats The Resistance: Avalon not as a game to be won but as a measurement instrument for Theory of Mind, decomposing ToM into a 2×2 taxonomy— 

Epistemic /Motivational mental states crossed with Inference /Action engagement—scored with human-authored, perspective-constrained state-ments whose labels are objectively verifiable, not inferred from outcomes. Benchmarking 28 models yields three findings. First, near-perfect rule recall coexists with far weaker perspective-constrained reasoning, so failures are deficits of social reason-ing, not missing knowledge. Second, the two axes are empirically dissociable—difficulty loads on the epistemic axis, the benefit of chain-of-thought on the orthogonal inference/action axis. Third, prob-ing, injection, and steering converge on one mech-anism: models represent the correct answer inter-nally but fail to express it, a gap closed far more by reasoning training ( +11 .0 pp) than by test-time chain-of-thought ( +1 .1 pp). Together these re-frame ToM failures as expression under a learned reasoning policy, and offer a diagnostic instrument for measuring progress. 8Limitations 

A single game. Every item is grounded in The Resistance: Avalon , whose hidden-role mechanics give clean, verifiable epistemic ground truth and a natural instantiation of all four taxonomy cells; but a single game cannot exhaust social reasoning. Whether the per-ability ordering and the represent-not-express gap transfer to other asymmetric-information settings—negotiation, multi-party dia-logue, other social-deduction games—is an empiri-cal question we do not settle here. 

Self-played games and a binary format. Items are drawn from games the authors played and recorded themselves and annotated post-hoc, rather than from independent human play at scale, trad-ing some ecological breadth for control and label certainty. The binary T RUE /F ALSE format that makes labels unambiguous also compresses ToM into a verification task, and does not measure a model’s ability to generate mental-state-aware be-havior in open-ended play. A few labels rest on benchmark-specific conventions (most notably the EVIL sabotage-priority protocol)—internally con-sistent design choices rather than universal facts. As a static set of 408 items across 97 scenarios with dimensions of unequal size, the benchmark is modest in scale and, like any fixed benchmark, subject to contamination as it circulates. 

Scope of the mechanistic claims. The prob-ing and steering analyses require access to in-ternal states and are run on the open-weight Qwen3 family; ground-truth injection extends the expression-gap claim behaviorally to all 28 models, including the proprietary frontier, but the causal, representation-level evidence is specific to mod-els we can open up. Probes are correlational, and steering is an existence proof that the answer is linearly writable, not a deployment recipe—it de-pends on a per-item sign from a supervised read-out, and a label-free variant helps only the smallest model. We localize the failure to expression un-der a learned reasoning policy but do not pinpoint where in training that policy forms.

C Extended Related Work 

This section expands the discussion in §2. 

Theory of Mind and its Evaluation in LLMs. 

Theory of Mind (ToM)—the capacity to at-tribute beliefs, desires, and intentions to others— originates in developmental psychology (Premack and Woodruff, 1978), where the false-belief paradigm established that reasoning about others’ 

wrong beliefs, including beliefs induced by de-ception, is the hallmark of mature ToM (Wimmer and Perner, 1983). As this framework extended to LLMs, early works often treated ToM as a mono-lithic capability, relying on short, bias-controlled question-answering as a proxy for social intelli-gence (Le et al., 2019). Subsequent benchmarks expanded along specific dimensions—interaction asymmetry in conversation (Kim et al., 2023), long-form narratives over varied psychological states (Xu et al., 2024a), fine-grained behavioral coverage (Chen et al., 2024), and higher-order story reason-ing (Wu et al., 2023). However, these evaluations frame ToM as a passive reading exercise that ig-nores dynamic, deceptive environments, and they treat mental-state reasoning as an entangled con-struct, failing to decouple epistemic knowledge from motivational intent. Thus, although adver-sarial stress tests have exposed the brittleness of LLM ToM (Shapira et al., 2024; Ullman, 2023), the difficulty of pinpointing exactly where reasoning breaks down—whether deficits stem from an in-ability to represent mental states versus an inability to express them—leaves open both why models fail and how these limitations can be mitigated. 

LLM Agents for Social Deduction Games. 

Hidden-role games have long served as testbeds for reasoning under information asymmetry. Pre-LLM systems built game-theoretic agents, e.g., Deep-Role’s counterfactual-regret planning for Avalon (Serrino et al., 2019) and Cicero’s human-level Diplomacy play (FAIR et al., 2022). With LLMs, a rapidly growing line of work builds agents that 

play social deduction games: AvalonBench pro-vides an Avalon environment with agent base-lines (Light et al., 2023); ReCon counters decep-tion through recursive contemplation (Wang et al., 2024); S TRATEGIST acquires strategic skills via bi-level tree search (Light et al., 2025); related agents target Werewolf (Xu et al., 2024b); and hy-brid frameworks that externalize belief inference to structured probabilistic models now defeat hu-12 man players (Rahimirad et al., 2026). Across this line, modeling what others know is instrumental —a means to win—and even when agents maintain explicit belief estimates, those estimates serve ac-tion selection and are validated chiefly by gameplay success. Win rate, however, conflates mental-state reasoning with planning, persuasion, and memory. In contrast, we treat Avalon not as a task to be solved but as a measurement instrument: every item is a verifiable binary judgment conditioned on a designated player’s epistemic state, so ToM is scored directly rather than inferred from game outcomes. 

Game-Based Evaluation of Social Reasoning. 

Closer to our goal, several efforts use games to eval-uate rather than to train players. GTBench mea-sures strategic reasoning through LLM-vs-LLM competition on game-theoretic tasks (Duan et al., 2024), and beauty-contest experiments estimate models’ depth of iterated reasoning (Lu, 2025); both target strategic competence rather than belief attribution. A complementary direction grounds evaluation in human play: Beyond Survival scores models against winning-faction strategies mined from a human-verified Werewolf corpus (Song et al., 2025), and InMind tests whether LLMs can capture and apply an individual player’s rea-soning style from annotated Avalon gameplay (Li et al., 2025). Such human-grounded evaluations offer ecological validity, but their gold labels are behavioral—alignment with what human players did or what the winning faction chose—so they measure fidelity to human play rather than the correctness of mental-state inference itself. Our benchmark occupies the complementary design point: expert-authored, perspective-constrained items with unambiguous epistemic ground truth, organized around the four ToM constructs of §3.1. 

Probing and Steering Internal Representations. 

A complementary interpretability literature reads and writes model computation directly rather than scoring outputs. Linear probes recover latent task information from hidden states (Alain and Bengio, 2017; Belinkov, 2022); models are shown to en-code knowledge their generations omit, including latent notions of truthfulness (Burns et al., 2023; Azaria and Mitchell, 2023); and activation steer-ing demonstrates that such directions are causal ,not merely correlational, letting targeted edits to the residual stream change model behavior (Turner et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2023). We bring these tools to ToM and use them to localize failure: prob-ing shows the correct mental-state inference is of-ten represented internally, and steering shows it is causally writable, together establishing that ToM limitations are primarily a problem of expression rather than representation—a mechanistic axis un-seen in prior ToM benchmarks.

H Prompt Details 

Every item is presented zero-shot with a two-part chat prompt: a fixed system message and a per-item user message. The gold label and reference rationale are never shown to the model. 

System message. The system message is the complete Avalon ruleset—objective, character abil-ities, the Lady-of-the-Lake mechanic, the phase structure, the three lineups, and the EVIL sabotage-priority convention—followed by the standing in-struction: “Reason only from the rules and visibil-ity constraints above plus the information given in each scenario. Do not assume knowledge a role cannot have.” This is identical across all items and models, so that role-visibility constraints are always in context and the task reduces to applying them from the designated seat. The ruleset content is the same as that summarized in Appendix D. 

User message. The user message is assembled from the item fields in a fixed order: 

> SETUP: {perspective-projected game state} WHAT HAS HAPPENED: {public history} Is the following statement a VALID, well-reasoned inference given the game’s information rules? STATEMENT: {statement} Answer True or False. End with a line: ’ANSWER: True’ or ’ANSWER: False’.

In the main condition the SETUP block is the com-plete annotated state G, including the full role as-signment . The model is thus handed god’s-eye information and is nonetheless expected to restrict its reasoning to what the designated seat can le-gitimately know, so that relying on inaccessible identities is penalized rather than rewarded (this is exactly the epistemic discipline the benchmark measures). L Chain-of-Thought Churn 

This section expands the churn analysis summa-rized in §5.2. The small net effect of toggling chain-of-thought on the same Qwen3 weights hides large per-item churn. Among items answered in both conditions, enabling chain-of-thought flips correct-ness on ∼27% of answers—roughly 16% fixed (wrong → right) and 12% broken (right → wrong)— so the modest net is fixes and breaks nearly can-celling (Figure 6). The smallest model breaks more than it fixes, and above ∼1B there is no monotonic relationship between model size and the net benefit. M Reasoning Length and ToM Accuracy 

This section expands the length analysis briefly referenced in §5.2. Within each Qwen3 model (thinking on), we bin responses into quartiles by generated length (Q1 shortest → Q4 longest) and measure accuracy per quartile. Accuracy does not increase from Q1 to Q4—if anything it declines (pooled corr( length , correct ) = −0.08 ; Figure 7). This relationship is partly confounded, since harder items tend to elicit longer responses, so we make only the weaker claim that response length is not a usable lever on ToM performance, not that longer reasoning causes errors.
