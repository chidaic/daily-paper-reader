# Tracking the Best Strategy in an Extensive-Form Game 

Stephen Pasteris spasteris@turing.ac.uk The Alan Turing Institute London, United Kingdom

## Abstract 

We consider the extensive-form bandit problem where on each trial the learner plays an extensive-form game against an oblivious adversary. We focus on the notion of switching regret, which measures the expected performance of the learner against that of any switching sequence of mixed strategies in retrospect. Our algorithm takes a parameter ρ > 0 and achieves a switching regret of ˜O((1 /ρ + ρK )√HAT ) where K is the number of switches in the comparator sequence, H is the maximum number of the learner’s information sets that can be traversed during a play of the game and A is the number of actions that the learner can possibly take. Our algorithm is extremely efficient, taking a per trial time of only O(HB ) where B is the maximum number of actions available to the learner at any of its information sets. 

## 1 Introduction 

We consider a (perfect recall) extensive form game. We call any mixed strategy of the learner a policy 

and any deterministic realisation of how the learner’s opponents (including any “chance” player) play an 

environment . The extensive-form bandit problem consists of T trials where on each trial we have an unknown environment μt fixed a-priori. On each trial t the learner stochastically plays the game against the enviroment 

μt , observing its information sets (a.k.a. infosets ) that are traversed. We denote the loss (i.e. the negated payoff) incurred by the learner on trial t by ℓt.The extensive-form bandit problem has been well studied by works such as Kozuno et al. (2021); Bai et al. (2022); Fiegel et al. (2022); Farina et al. (2021b); Maiti et al. (2025) which give bounds on the static regret ,which is the cumulative loss incurred by the learner minus that which it would have obtained by always playing according to the best fixed policy in retrospect. In this paper we focus on the more general notion of switching regret , which is the cumulative loss incurred by the learner minus that which would have been expected by playing an arbitrary sequence of policies in retrospect. Formally, given a policy π and an environment μ′, we define Λ( π, μ ′) to be the expected loss incurred by the learner if it were to play the policy 

π against the environment μ′. Given a sequence of policies ϑ ∈ P T we define the (switching) regret with respect to ϑ as:

1.1 Related Work 

The notion of switching regret was introduced in Herbster & Warmuth (1995), which, generalising the Hedge 

algorithm of Freund & Schapire (1997), gave the algorithm FixedShare for the problem of prediction with expert advice. FixedShare was modified by Auer et al. (2002) to create the Exp3.S algorithm for non-stationary learning in the adversarial bandit problem. In the case in which the learner has a single infoset, our problem reduces to the adversarial bandit problem and our algorithm TrackEFG reduces to Exp3.S .The work Maiti et al. (2025) reduces the extensive-form bandit problem to adversarial online shortest path on a directed acyclic graph under semi-bandit feedback. For this problem the works Benkö et al. (2007); Vural et al. (2020) give switching regret guarantees. We suspect that a refined analysis of Benkö et al. (2007), specific to extensive-form games, would lead to a regret of: 

E[R(ϑ)] ∈ ˜O

(( 1

where S is the (massive) number of the learner’s reduced strategies in the reduced strategic form of the game and X is the maximum number of leaves that are reachable under any environment. Whilst this bound would be, in general, incomparable to ours, the algorithm has an extremely high per-trial time and space complexity of O(AT ). The work Vural et al. (2020) gives an algorithm with a regret of: 

E[R(ϑ)] ∈ ˜O

(( 1

where Y is the maximum number of the learner’s infosets that are reachable under any of the learner’s pure strategies. Not only is this regret bound significantly worse than ours but the algorithm has an extremely high per-trial time complexity of O(A4). One may wonder whether the corralling machinery of Luo et al. (2022) could be applied to BalancedOMD 

Bai et al. (2022) which efficiently obtains static regret bounds for the extensive-form bandit problem. How-ever, there are issues in doing this. The first issue is that, to work with Luo et al. (2022), Exp3 had to be modified which means that BalancedOMD , which generalises Exp3 , would also need to be modified. The second issue is that, as far as we are aware, on any trial, the bias term would be dependent on the entire environment at that trial, which is unknown at the end of the trial. We believe that the bias term could be overestimated but this would lead to a dramatically worse regret bound than us and take a time of O(A) to compute. We believe the same issues to hold when trying to corral the algorithms of Kozuno et al. (2021); Fiegel et al. (2022). With regard to the algorithms of Maiti et al. (2025); Farina et al. (2021b), even if these algorithms could be corralled, they would suffer from a dramatically higher regret and time complexity than 

TrackEFG .2Whilst the works Kozuno et al. (2021); Bai et al. (2022) are based on dilated mirror descent Hoda et al. (2010); Farina et al. (2021a), there is also a line of work Lanctot et al. (2009); Farina et al. (2020); Bai et al. (2022) on monte-carlo counterfactual regret minimisation . However, whilst achieving the goal of finding a Nash equilibrium (for a two player zero-sum game), these works fail to give a bound on the true static regret, as the game is played with a policy that is different from the policy that the “regret” is measured against. The work Noarov et al. (2023) considered the notion of subsequence regret, in which switching regret can be seen as a special case. However, their algorithm is full information (in that it must see the entirety of the environment at the end of each trial) and their external regret bound is at least ˜O(A√T ) which is high -especially for a full information algorithm. The per-trial time complexity of their algorithm is also at least 

O(T 2). Related to the extensive-form bandit problem is the problem of reinforcement learning (where transitions are stochastic rather than adversarial). The work Wei & Luo (2021) studied reinforcement learning where the transition probabilities change gradually over time. The development and analysis of TrackEFG was inspired by the works Pasteris et al. (2023); Herbster & Warmuth (1995); Bai et al. (2022); Pasteris et al. (2026).

## 2 The Game 

For this paper we need not fully define an imperfect information extensive-form game as we only need to focus on the leaner’s infosets and actions and not those of its opponents. In this one-sided view of the game we have a rooted tree whose nodes are either an infoset of the learner, an action of the learner, or a terminal node (a.k.a. leaf ). The children of each infoset are the set of actions that are avaliable at that infoset and the children of each action are the possible leaves or infosets (of the learner) that can be encountered after that action is taken. Formally, we have a rooted tree and a function λ : L → [0 , 1] where L is the set of leaves of the tree. Let V

be the set of nodes of the tree and let r be the root of the tree. Given a node v ∈ V , let C(v) be the set of its children and, given v̸ = r, let p(v) be its parent. The set V \ L , of internal nodes of the tree, is partitioned into two sets N (the set of the learner’s infosets ) and A (the set of the learner’s actions ) satisfying the following rules: • r ∈ N .• For all v ∈ N we have C(v) ⊆ A .• For all a ∈ A we have C(a) ⊆ N ∪ L .Let H be the maximum number of nodes in A in any root-to-leaf path. We note that, in this paper, we will, without loss of generality, consider only deterministic environments, as to handle stochastic environments one simply draws an environment from a probability distribution (so our regret bound will hold for stochastic environments as well). ## 3 The Problem and Result 

Our problem consists of T trials where on each trial we have an unknown (to the learner) environment μt

fixed a-priori. The learner knows the tree a-priori but not necessarily the function λ. For each trial t ∈ [T ]in turn the learner plays the game against μt. Let ℓt be the loss incurred by the learner on trial t. The aim of the learner is to minimise the cumulative loss incurred. In this paper we give an algorithm TrackEFG for the learner that takes a parameter ρ > 2/√T . In order to present our main theorem we make the following definitions.
